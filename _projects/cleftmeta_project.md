## Purpose

**Nonsyndromic cleft palate**, with or without cleft lip (often shortened to **NSCP/L**), is a common birth difference where the roof of the mouth does not fully close during early development. Because the palate plays a critical role in building oral air pressure and shaping speech sounds, children with NSCP/L are at increased risk for delays in **speech sound development**. Speech skills are foundational for learning **language** (words, grammar, conversation) and later **reading**.

Over the last several decades, many researchers have studied speech, language, and reading development in children with NSCP/L. However, findings have often been **inconsistent**—with some studies reporting clear delays and others suggesting that children “catch up” over time. Our goal was to resolve these inconsistencies by using **meta-analysis**, a research method that combines results across many studies to estimate overall patterns of development.

---

## What is a meta-analysis?

A **meta-analysis** is a research method that combines and summarizes results from multiple studies that ask similar questions. Rather than relying on a single study, meta-analysis allows researchers to:

- Estimate the *average* difference between groups across many samples  
- Improve confidence in results by increasing sample size  
- Examine why results might differ across studies  

We followed the **PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses)** framework to ensure a transparent and rigorous review process.

---

## How we did it

### Finding the studies

We conducted comprehensive literature searches across multiple databases and hand-reviewed reference lists. Across the full project, we screened **thousands of records** and identified **31 eligible studies** that compared children with NSCP/L to children without clefts. To be included, studies had to:

- Include children with **nonsyndromic** cleft palate with or without cleft lip  
- Include a **non-cleft comparison group**  
- Measure **speech, language, and/or reading skills**  
- Include children from **birth through early school age**  

Studies that met criteria were included in one or more of our meta-analyses depending on the skills reported.

### What skills did we examine?

For group comparisons, we calculated standardized effect sizes (Hedges’ *g*) to estimate differences between children with and without NSCP/L for the following areas:

- **Independent Speech**  
  The range of speech sounds a child can produce, regardless of accuracy (e.g., consonant inventories from speech samples).

- **Speech Accuracy**  
  How closely a child’s speech matches adult targets (e.g., percent consonants correct from single-word or connected speech tasks).

- **Speech Errors**  
  The presence of speech sound errors, including cleft-related patterns and developmental errors.

- **Expressive Language**  
  Language used to communicate ideas (e.g., vocabulary size, sentence length, standardized expressive language tests, parent report, language samples).

- **Receptive Language**  
  Language used to understand spoken messages (e.g., receptive vocabulary, following directions, comprehension measures).

- **General Reading**  
  Broad reading skills such as word reading, reading fluency, and comprehension.

- **Pre-Reading**  
  Skills required to learn to read such as print awareness, phonological awareness (rhyming, blending, segmentation), and nonword repetition.

---

### Relationships between speech and language

In a subset of studies, we also examined how **early speech skills and language skills are related** within children with NSCP/L by meta-analyzing correlations between:

- **Consonant inventory** and **vocabulary size**  
- **Consonant inventory** and **mean length of utterance (MLU)**  

These analyses allowed us to ask whether children who have stronger speech sound skills also tend to have stronger language skills.

---

### What factors might explain differences?

We coded several **moderators**, or factors that could influence results across studies, including:

- **Child characteristics** (e.g., age, cleft type)  
- **Assessment characteristics** (e.g., speech sampled from single words vs. conversation; standardized tests vs. parent report)  
- **Study characteristics** (e.g., publication year, country)

---

## What we learned

### Delays in speech

Across studies, young children with NSCP/L showed **large and persistent delays in speech development** compared to children without clefts{% cite lancaster_early_2020 %}. On average, children with NSCP/L had:

- **smaller consonant inventories**  
- **lower speech accuracy**  
- **more speech sound errors**  

Speech differences were evident from toddlerhood through early school age.

### Delays in language

Children with NSCP/L also demonstrated **moderate delays in expressive and receptive language** {% cite lancaster_early_2020 %}. Expressive language differences tended to **decrease with age**, especially when language was measured using standardized tests. However, receptive language differences remained more consistent over time.

### Delays in reading

School-age children with NSCP/L showed **subtle but meaningful weaknesses in reading** compared to peers {% cite lancaster_reading_2021 %}, particularly in:

- **pre-reading skills**, such as phonological awareness  
- **word decoding skills**  

These findings suggest that early speech and language differences may place some children with NSCP/L at risk for later reading difficulties.

### A meaningful link between speech and language

We found a **moderate-to-strong relationship between early speech sound development and expressive language**, especially vocabulary {% cite lien_meta-analysis_2024 %}. Children with larger consonant inventories tended to have larger vocabularies. This suggests that early speech skills may serve as an important **marker of language risk** in children with NSCP/L.

---

## Why this work matters

Together, these meta-analyses show that children with NSCP/L are **at increased risk across speech, language, and reading development**, even when average scores fall within the “normal” range on standardized tests. Monitoring multiple areas of communication—and especially early speech development—may support earlier identification of children who could benefit from targeted intervention.
